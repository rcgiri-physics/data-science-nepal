# How to use this book

## The shape of every chapter

Each of the 40 chapters (8 per grade) follows the same pattern, so you always know where you are:

| Part of the chapter | What it is for |
|---|---|
| **Header** | track (A no-code / B code), tools, number of lessons, datasets used |
| **Big idea** | the one sentence to remember |
| **What you will be able to do** | "I can…" goals — use them to check yourself at the end |
| **Words to know** | key terms, with Nepali where established (full list in Appendix B) |
| **Before you start** | what you need from earlier chapters |
| **Lessons** (2–5) | each = one class period of 40–45 minutes |
| ↳ *Hook* | a local question to think about — predict before reading on |
| ↳ *Explanation* | the idea, worked examples, code where relevant |
| ↳ *Activity* | what to do, step by step (paper and computer versions) |
| ↳ *Common mistakes* | what most people get wrong |
| ↳ *Check* | three quick questions |
| ↳ *Reflect* | "What surprised me? Who or what is missing from this data?" |
| **Unit summary** | the key points on one screen |
| **Practice exercises** | ★ recall · ★★ apply · ★★★ think deeper |
| **Exercises with real data** | using free datasets (codes RD-01 … RD-20, Appendix A) with an offline fallback |
| **Unit project** | a small investigation following the PPDAC cycle |
| **Going further** | free resources for the curious |

## If you are a student learning on your own

1. **Go in order.** Grades build on each other; each chapter's *Before you start* tells you what you need.
2. **Predict before you read.** Every hook asks a question. Write your guess down. Being wrong first is how you learn.
3. **Do, don't just read.** Work through every worked example yourself — with a pencil for Grade 8, with the computer
   from Grade 9. Type the code; don't copy and paste it.
4. **Do the Check questions without looking back.** Then check. This "retrieval practice" is one of the most powerful
   study methods known (see *After Grade 12: how to study*).
5. **Do at least the ★ and ★★ exercises.** Answers to every ★ exercise are in Appendix G (student edition); the
   teacher edition has answers to all exercises. With a friend, swap and check each other's work.
6. **Do the real-data exercises** whenever you have internet; they turn practice into real discoveries about Nepal.
7. **Do the unit project.** Projects are where you put the pieces together. Keep them in a folder: by Grade 12 you will
   have a portfolio.
8. **Use AI assistants as tutors, not as answer machines** (Grade 10 Unit 6): ask for explanations and hints, disclose
   what you used, and make sure you could explain everything yourself.

A realistic self-study pace is **one lesson per day** or **one chapter per week**.

## If you are a teacher

1. Read **Part: Teaching data-science foundations**, at least chapters 1 (why and what), 4 (a good lesson) and 7
   (low-resource classrooms).
2. Choose your **track**:
   * **Track A (no-code)** — paper, calculator, spreadsheet or CODAP. Fits inside compulsory Maths, Science, Social
     Studies or Health. Grade 8 is entirely Track A; many Grade 9–12 lessons have a Track A version.
   * **Track B (code)** — SQL and Python (JupyterLite in the browser works offline). Fits optional Computer Science,
     ICT clubs and Grades 11–12.
3. Use the **teacher guide** for each unit (teacher edition): timings, materials, misconceptions, differentiation,
   answer keys for Checks, practice and real-data exercises.
4. **Pace realistically.** Each unit = 3–5 lessons + a project (about 2 periods). A full grade = 8 units ≈ 40 periods,
   one period a week for a year, or two a week for a term. See *Planning a year* in the teaching part for options when
   you have less time.
5. **Adapt freely.** Change examples to your district, swap datasets, translate. The licence allows it; please share
   improvements.

## Synthetic and real data

Practice tables in the `datasets/` folder are **synthetic**: invented numbers, shaped like real Nepali data, with
fictional district names such as `Hill-07`. They let every lesson run offline and let the book show exact answers. **Never
quote them as facts about Nepal.** Real data appear in the *Exercises with real data*, downloaded from the publisher (see
Appendix A), and in the worked examples where a publisher's licence allows it (for example, child mortality from Our
World in Data in Grade 8 Unit 4).

## Symbols and conventions

* ★ / ★★ / ★★★ — exercise difficulty.
* **RD-05** — real dataset number 5 in Appendix A.
* `code` — something to type, a file name or a column name.
* Code blocks in chapters are tested automatically: they run as printed on the shipped synthetic data.
* Dates are written in ISO form (2026-10-11) unless a Bikram Sambat (BS) date is meant, which is marked "BS".

## Two editions

* **Student edition** — everything above, without the teacher guides, plus answers to the ★ exercises (Appendix G).
* **Teacher edition** — adds each unit's teacher guide (timings, misconceptions, answers to all exercises and notes on the
