# 1. Why teach data science at school — and what "foundation" means

## Why now, why here

* **Data shape decisions about Nepal.** Census results decide seats and budgets; health-post records decide where
  vaccines go; rainfall and river data decide flood warnings; remittance and price data shape household lives. Citizens
  who cannot read data critically can be misled; citizens who can, can hold decisions to account.
* **The school curriculum already touches data, but thinly.** Grade 8 Maths teaches mean, median, mode and pie charts;
  optional Computer Science in Grades 9–10 introduces databases and Python; NEB Grades 11–12 Computer Science covers
  databases and programming. What is mostly missing is the **investigative** core: forming questions, collecting fairly,
  judging uncertainty, interpreting honestly and thinking about people and fairness.
* **AI tools are now everywhere.** Students who understand how models learn from data, why they make mistakes and whom
  they can harm will use AI more wisely — and some will build better tools for Nepal.
* **Jobs and further study.** Data skills are valued in government, health, banking, development, agriculture,
  journalism, research and technology, in Nepal and abroad.

## What "data science foundations" are

Not a list of software commands. Five strands that grow together:

1. **Statistical thinking** — variability is everywhere; questions about groups; distributions; comparing fairly;
   uncertainty; the difference between pattern and cause.
2. **Data handling** — tidy data, cleaning, documentation, databases, reproducibility.
3. **Computational tools** — spreadsheets, SQL, Python, from unplugged to code.
4. **Modelling** — from a line of best fit to honest machine learning with baselines and validation.
5. **Communication and ethics** — claims with evidence and limits; consent, privacy, fairness, who is missing.

A student has a **foundation** when they can run a small investigation from question to honest conclusion, in any of
the tools they know, and can explain each step. The following chapter shows how each strand climbs from Grade 8 to
Grade 12.

## Principles behind this curriculum

* **Investigation first, tools second.** The PPDAC cycle (Problem, Plan, Data, Analysis, Conclusion) is the same from
  Grade 8 to 12; tools are added only when the thinking needs them.
* **Spiral, not silo.** The same ideas return each year at greater depth (rates in Grades 8, 9 and 12; uncertainty from
  "is a bigger sample better?" in Grade 8 to the bootstrap in Grade 11).
* **Local data and contexts.** Districts, belts, monsoon, air quality, migration; synthetic tables shaped like Nepal;
  real public data for exercises.
* **Low-tech first.** Every concept has an unplugged or paper version. Computers deepen learning; they are not a
  prerequisite for starting.
* **Simulation before formulas.** Shuffling slips and drawing samples builds intuition that formulas then summarise.
* **Honesty and people.** Every lesson ends with "Who or what is missing from this data?"
* **Open and adaptable.** CC BY 4.0: translate, localise, print, improve.

## Where it fits in the timetable

| Host | How |
|---|---|
| **Compulsory Maths (Grade 8–10)** | Track A lessons replace or extend the statistics chapters (Grade 8 Units 1–7; Grade 9 Units 1, 2, 7; Grade 10 Units 2–4 on paper) |
| **Science** | measurement, variability, experiments, climate and air (Grade 8 Units 2–5; Grade 10 Units 3–4; Grade 11 Units 3–4) |
| **Social Studies / Health** | census, migration, health data, ethics (Grade 8 Units 6–8; Grade 9 Unit 7; Grade 10 Unit 6; Grade 12 Units 5, 7) |
| **Optional Computer Science (Grade 9–10)** | Track B units: SQL, Python, pandas, dashboards |
| **NEB Computer Science (Grade 11–12)** | enrichment alongside DBMS and programming units |
| **Clubs, extra-curricular, summer camps** | projects and capstones |
